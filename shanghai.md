# Shanghai, China

## Pitch
The most international city in Asia — art deco Bund architecture, the world's fastest train, and a food scene that spans 1,000 years of Chinese culinary tradition.

## Overview
Shanghai is China's most cosmopolitan city and its most immediately navigable for first-time visitors. The Bund — a mile-long waterfront promenade of 1920s and 30s colonial-era buildings facing the Pudong skyline — is one of the great urban vistas in the world: art deco on one side, science fiction on the other. The city is enormous (24 million people) but organized around neighborhoods that are human in scale: the French Concession is leafy and beautiful, full of plane trees and boutiques; the Old City and Yu Garden recall imperial China; Tianzifang is a warren of converted shikumen alleyways with galleries and cafés.

For a large family group, Shanghai offers the clearest on-ramp to China. The infrastructure is exceptional — the metro is clean, cheap, and extensive; the airport maglev train reaches 430km/h and connects to the city in 8 minutes; DiDi covers everything else. Mandarin speakers in the group unlock a meaningfully richer experience, but Shanghai's tourist infrastructure is among the most English-accessible in mainland China. Day trips by high-speed rail reach Suzhou (30 min) and Hangzhou (45 min), both UNESCO-level destinations in their own right.

## Culture
Shanghai has a cultural identity distinct from the rest of China — it was shaped as much by its foreign concession history (British, French, American, Japanese sectors operated semi-independently until 1943) as by Chinese tradition. The result is a city that absorbed modernism earlier and more thoroughly than anywhere else in China. The art deco architecture, the jazz-age café culture, the fashion-forward residents — all of it reflects a century of unusual hybridity. Shanghainese are known within China for being sophisticated, fast-paced, and slightly status-conscious. The city moves quickly.

Chinese culture applies fully: children are adored, grandparents are treated with great respect, and a large family group will be received warmly everywhere. Maggie's Mandarin unlocks menus, negotiations, local tips, and the meaningful difference between the tourist version of Shanghai and the actual one.

## History
Shanghai's modern history is compressed and dramatic. The city was a modest fishing town until the First Opium War (1842) forced it open to foreign trade, and within 50 years it was the most important port in Asia. The foreign concessions created architectural layers that are still visible — the Bund, the French Concession, the art deco apartment blocks of the former International Settlement. The 1930s were Shanghai's gilded age: Noël Coward finished Private Lives at the Cathay Hotel (now the Fairmont Peace Hotel) in 1930. The Communist takeover in 1949 closed the city to foreign influence for 40 years; the reopening in the 1990s produced the Pudong skyline, built almost entirely in a decade.

The contrast between the colonial Bund and the futuristic Pudong, separated only by the Huangpu River, is Shanghai's defining visual argument: old and new existing in direct confrontation, neither apologizing.

## Nature
Shanghai is flat, urban, and dense — nature is not the draw. The Huangpu River is the organizing spine. Century Park in Pudong is large and pleasant. The real nature payoff is in the day trips: Hangzhou's West Lake (a UNESCO landscape of causeways, pagodas, and hills reflected in still water) is 45 minutes by bullet train and one of the most beautiful places in China. Suzhou's classical gardens — listed collectively as UNESCO World Heritage — are jewels of Chinese garden design, intricate and extraordinary.

## Food
Shanghai's food scene spans everything from 1,000-year-old Chinese culinary traditions to contemporary fine dining that rivals Tokyo and Paris. The local cuisine — Shanghainese — is milder and sweeter than Sichuan, using more soy, sugar, and slow-braising. Xiaolongbao (soup dumplings) are the signature dish: paper-thin dough, pork and crab filling, a pocket of hot soup inside each one. Eat them at Din Tai Fung (Taiwanese export but excellent here) or better at Jia Jia Tang Bao on Huanghe Road. Shengjianbao (pan-fried pork buns) from Yang's Fry-Dumpling are a different but equally essential experience.

Beyond Shanghainese: the city has exceptional Cantonese, Sichuan, Hunan, and Yunnan restaurants. The French Concession has an outstanding international dining scene for the adults who want a break from Chinese food. Ultraviolet by Paul Pairet — 10-seat, single-table experience projected into a multi-sensory environment — is one of the most extraordinary dining experiences in the world; book months in advance.

For the group: xiaolongbao and shengjianbao are universally beloved including by kids. The dim sum culture here is excellent. Anyone who eats Chinese food at home will find plenty to love.

## For Kids
- Shanghai Disney Resort — the newest and arguably most beautifully designed Disney park in the world. Opened 2016; the Enchanted Storybook Castle is the largest in any Disney park. Full day minimum. Book in advance for peak periods.
- Maglev train from Pudong airport — the world's fastest commercial train, reaching 430km/h in 4 minutes. Ride it on arrival from the airport. Kids will want to ride it again.
- Yu Garden and the Old City — a 16th-century classical garden embedded in a bustling bazaar. Zigzag bridges over koi ponds, pavilions, rockeries. The surrounding bazaar sells everything from silk to soup dumplings.
- Shanghai Natural History Museum — exceptional natural history museum with a dinosaur hall, an evolution spiral, and exhibits scaled for children. Better than most natural history museums in the US.
- Tianzifang — a maze of converted shikumen alleyways in the French Concession with art galleries, craft shops, and food stalls. Easy for kids to wander and explore.

## For the Family Group
- DiDi (Chinese Uber) handles all in-city transport — no cars, no navigation, no parking. Essential app; Maggie can set it up for the group.
- Maglev train from PVG airport to Longyang Road metro station (8 min, 430km/h) then metro to hotel — the most thrilling airport transfer in the world. Do this on arrival.
- Both LAX and SEA have nonstop flights with similar flight times — clean symmetric routing for the group.
- Mandarin speakers in the group (Maggie) unlock a qualitatively better experience everywhere: real menus vs. tourist menus, negotiating at markets, local restaurant recommendations that don't appear on any English-language guide.
- Visa planning: Maggie/Bill/Jordan already hold 10-year China visas. Extended family members need to apply for tourist L visas 4-6 weeks in advance.

## For Adults
- Ultraviolet by Paul Pairet — 10-seat restaurant where each course is accompanied by projected visuals, scent, and sound synchronized to the dish. One of the most unusual and extraordinary dining experiences in the world. Book months ahead.
- The Bund at night — the illuminated colonial buildings reflected in the Huangpu River, with the Pudong skyline behind. Walk it twice: once in the day, once at night.
- Vue bar at the Hyatt on the Bund — panoramic rooftop bar with arguably the best Bund view in Shanghai. Go at dusk.
- Xintiandi — a restored shikumen neighborhood converted into upscale dining and bars. Where the Chinese Communist Party held its first congress in 1921; now surrounded by restaurants.
- Antique markets: Dongtai Road Antique Market for browsing; Jing'an antique market for better-quality finds. Maggie's Mandarin is essential for negotiating.

## Day Trips
- **Suzhou** — 30 min by bullet train. The "Venice of the East" — canals, classical gardens (Humble Administrator's Garden, Lingering Garden), and silk weaving. UNESCO-listed gardens are among the finest in China. Full day.
- **Hangzhou + West Lake** — 45 min by bullet train. West Lake is one of China's most celebrated landscapes — causeways, pagodas, tea plantations on surrounding hills, rowing boats on the lake. Full day; overnight if the group wants more.
- **Zhujiajiao water town** — 1 hr by car or bus. A well-preserved Ming and Qing dynasty canal town with stone bridges, gondola rides, and local food stalls. Half day; less touristy than some alternatives.

## Cost
**M — Medium.** Shanghai is expensive by Chinese standards but genuinely affordable by international luxury standards. Anchor hotels (Peninsula Shanghai, Waldorf Astoria on the Bund) run $500-900/night in peak season — premium for the Bund view but reasonable for the quality. Mid-range French Concession hotels run $200-400/night. Flights are competitive — United and Air China nonstops from both LAX and SEA run ~$800-1,100 roundtrip. Food is a major value: xiaolongbao from Jia Jia Tang Bao costs $3; a full Shanghainese dinner at a good restaurant is $25-40/person. Disney and Ultraviolet are the significant activity cost exceptions. Verify hotel rates at booking.

## Group Logistics
The Peninsula Shanghai (The Bund, Zhongshan East Road) is the anchor — 235 rooms, directly on the Bund with river views, exceptional service, and a location that puts the Old City and French Concession within walking or short DiDi distance. The Waldorf Astoria Shanghai on the Bund (converted 1910 Shanghai Club building) is the atmospheric alternative — smaller, more historic, equally well-located.

For 10+ people: both properties accommodate large groups with multi-room configurations. Book well in advance for autumn (September-November is peak season). Consider a French Concession base (The Middle House, URBN Hotel) as a mid-range alternative — quieter neighborhood, excellent walkability, slightly lower rates.

DiDi is the primary transport — Maggie sets it up and it covers the group for the full trip. Metro is excellent for the group if comfortable navigating Chinese-language signage (major stations have English). No car rental needed.
