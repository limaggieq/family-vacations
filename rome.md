# Rome, Italy

## Pitch
The entire ancient world in one city — the Colosseum, the Vatican, the Pantheon — and every dinner is an event.

## Overview
Rome is not a city you run out of. It has been continuously inhabited for nearly 3,000 years and the accumulation of that history is everywhere: temples embedded in medieval churches, classical fountains at the center of Baroque piazzas, ancient roads still in use. The Colosseum is 2,000 years old and still the largest amphitheater ever built. The Pantheon's dome was the largest in the world for 1,300 years and its concrete has never been repaired. Walking Rome is walking through layers of time in a way that no other city on earth replicates.

For a large family group, Rome works because it is inherently spectacular at every level. Kids don't need context to understand the Colosseum — the scale does the work. Adults don't need a guide to appreciate a plate of cacio e pepe in a trattoria that's been open since 1890. The city is compact enough that the main sites are walkable from a single central hotel, and the day-trip infrastructure — to the Amalfi Coast, to Florence, to Pompeii — is excellent by train. A week in Rome doesn't require a car, a connection, or a complicated plan.

## Culture
Romans are expressive, warm, and direct in a way that differs meaningfully from northern European formality. The city moves at its own pace — lunch closes for two hours in the afternoon, restaurants don't open for dinner until 7:30pm, and no one hurries. For a large American family group, this pace is either the best part of the trip or a logistical adjustment depending on temperament. The food culture is deeply serious: a Roman will have strong opinions about where you should eat cacio e pepe, and they are usually right. Dress modestly when entering churches — bare shoulders and shorts will get you turned away from the Vatican and major basilicas.

Italian is the working language everywhere. English is widely spoken in tourist areas; outside them, basic Italian phrases go a long way and are received with genuine warmth.

## History
Rome's history is so dense that a week barely scratches the surface. The Roman Republic was founded in 509 BC; the Empire at its height controlled territory from Scotland to Mesopotamia. The sack of Rome in 410 AD was so shocking that Augustine wrote The City of God in response. The Renaissance papacy rebuilt the city in the 15th and 16th centuries — Michelangelo painted the Sistine Chapel ceiling between 1508 and 1512, on his back, over four years. Bernini designed St. Peter's Square in the 17th century. The city has been a living museum of Western civilization for twenty centuries, and the weight of that is palpable on the street.

## Nature
Rome is urban and warm rather than dramatically natural. The city's parks — Villa Borghese, the Pincian Hill gardens, the Aventine Hill — are beautiful in the formal Italian mode. The Tiber river runs through the center but is not the Seine; the riverbanks are more atmospheric than scenic.

The real nature payoff is in the day trips. The Amalfi Coast — dramatic limestone cliffs, turquoise water, cliffside villages — is 3 hours south. Pompeii (2.5 hours south) is buried under Vesuvius's ash and is one of the most extraordinary archaeological sites in the world. The Castelli Romani hill towns (45 minutes southeast) offer cool, forested terrain and lake swimming in summer.

## Food
Roman cuisine is one of the great regional Italian traditions — peasant food elevated to art. The five canonical Roman pasta dishes: cacio e pepe (pecorino and black pepper), carbonara (egg, guanciale, pecorino), amatriciana (guanciale, tomato, pecorino), gricia (guanciale, pecorino), and aglio e olio (garlic and olive oil). Every trattoria does at least two of these well; the best do all five perfectly. Beyond pasta: supplì (fried rice balls with tomato and mozzarella, eaten standing on the street), artichokes alla Romana or alla giudia (Jewish-style deep fried), saltimbocca, abbacchio (Roman spring lamb). Pizza al taglio — rectangular pizza sold by weight — is the lunch staple.

For the group: Roman food is universally beloved. Kids eat pasta. Adults eat everything else. The only challenge is pacing — the meal is long and that is the point.

Specific standouts: Roscioli (deli and restaurant, exceptional cacio e pepe), Da Enzo al 29 (Trastevere trattoria, book ahead), Flavio al Velavevodetto (testaccio, classic Roman), Supplì Roma (best supplì in the city), Giolitti (gelato, operating since 1900).

## For Kids
- Colosseum — the scale is incomprehensible until you're inside it. Book gladiator experience tickets in advance; kids put on a costume and "fight" in the arena. Theatrical and memorable.
- Vatican Museums + Sistine Chapel — even a 5-year-old understands that Michelangelo painted the ceiling lying on his back. The scale of the Sistine Chapel is genuinely awe-inspiring. Book timed entry well in advance.
- Trevi Fountain — throw a coin over your left shoulder with your right hand. Legend says you'll return to Rome. Jordan will want to throw many coins.
- Borghese Gallery — the most beautiful small museum in Rome. Bernini's sculptures (Daphne transforming into a tree, Pluto grabbing Persephone) look impossible. Requires advance booking; max 2 hours per visit by design.
- Explora — Rome's children's museum, designed for ages 3-12. Good for an afternoon when the kids need a break from ancient ruins.

## For the Family Group
- Rome's centro storico (historic center) is compact and largely walkable — the Colosseum, Roman Forum, Palatine Hill, Trevi Fountain, Pantheon, Piazza Navona, and the Vatican are all within 30-40 minutes on foot of each other.
- No car needed for the entire week. Taxis and walking cover the city; trains cover day trips.
- Italian culture is multigenerationally warm. Children are adored everywhere — in restaurants, in shops, on the street. Grandparents are treated with great respect. This destination works for every age in the group.
- Evening passeggiata (the ritual evening walk) is a natural group activity — dress up slightly, join the crowds on Via del Corso or around the Pantheon, eat gelato, repeat.
- Book Vatican and Colosseum timed entry as early as possible — these sell out weeks ahead in shoulder season and months ahead in summer.

## For Adults
- Roscioli deli and restaurant — arguably the best cacio e pepe in Rome. Also a wine shop with an exceptional cellar. Book the restaurant table weeks ahead.
- Borghese Gallery — book well in advance (limited to 360 visitors per 2-hour slot). Bernini's marble sculptures are among the most extraordinary objects ever made.
- Wine: Frascati (the local Castelli Romani white, drunk cold in summer), Cesanese (the local red), and anything from the sommelier at Roscioli or Il Sorì wine bar.
- Campo de' Fiori evening — the square shifts from a morning produce market to an aperitivo scene by evening. Negroni at a terrace bar as the light goes gold.
- Day trip to Pompeii — one of the most important archaeological sites in the world. 2.5 hours by train from Roma Termini. A full day minimum.

## Day Trips
- **Pompeii + Herculaneum** — 2.5 hrs by train (Frecciarossa to Naples, then Circumvesuviana local). Pompeii is vast; Herculaneum is smaller and better preserved. Do one or both. Full day.
- **Amalfi Coast** — 3 hrs by train to Salerno or Naples, then ferry or bus along the coast. Best as an overnight to Positano or Ravello. The coast itself is one of the world's great natural spectacles.
- **Florence** — 1.5 hrs by Frecciarossa high-speed train. The Uffizi, the Duomo, the Ponte Vecchio, the David. Full day or overnight.
- **Castelli Romani** — 45 min by train from Termini. Hill towns (Frascati, Castel Gandolfo), lake swimming at Lago Albano, wine tasting. Easy half-day.
- **Orvieto** — 1 hr by train. A medieval city on a volcanic cliff with a cathedral whose façade is one of the finest examples of Italian Gothic architecture. Half day.

## Cost
**M-H — Medium-High.** Rome is moderately expensive by European capital standards. Anchor hotels (Hotel de Russie, Palazzo Manfredi) run $700-1,200/night in peak season; good mid-range options in Trastevere or the centro storico run $300-500/night. Flights are competitive — multiple carriers on the LAX route (~$800-1,100 roundtrip); the SEA direct is seasonal and budget-carrier operated, which keeps prices lower (~$600-900) but requires schedule verification. Food is a genuine value — a full trattoria dinner with wine runs $50-80/person; pizza al taglio and supplì cost almost nothing. Museums run $18-25/person; Vatican requires advance booking at similar prices. Verify hotel rates at booking.

## Group Logistics
Hotel de Russie (Via del Babuino, between Piazza del Popolo and the Spanish Steps) is the anchor — 122 rooms, terraced garden, central location. Palazzo Manfredi (adjacent to the Colosseum, rooftop terrace with direct Colosseum views) is the more dramatic option for a smaller group. For 10+ people, the de Russie is the practical choice; supplement with the adjacent Hotel Locarno or Hotel Piranesi if additional rooms are needed.

For Trastevere-based alternatives: the neighborhood is quieter, more local, and excellent for families with young kids — Villa della Fonte and Donna Camilla Savelli are both good smaller properties. Trastevere is 20 minutes on foot or a short tram ride from the main sites.

No car needed within Rome. Train from FCO airport to Roma Termini runs every 30 minutes (32 min, €14/person). For day trips: Frecciarossa high-speed trains from Termini are fast and reliable — book in advance on Trenitalia.
